The Bee in Human Symbolism

From a honey hunter painted on a Spanish cliff eight thousand years ago to the worker bee tattooed across Manchester in 2017: how one insect became a sign for sovereignty, the soul, industry, prophecy and, most recently, the health of the living world.

The Honey Hunters

Before writing

Rock walls, smoke and rope: the bee as danger and reward

c. 8000–6000 BCEBicorp, Valencia, IberiaContestedFertility & renewal

The Man on the Rope

The oldest surviving picture of a human being taking honey is painted on a rock shelter wall in eastern Spain. A figure hangs from plaited ropes over a void, reaches into a crevice, and is surrounded by a scatter of small marks: the bees. Beside the figure hangs a basket for the comb.

What the painter chose to record is not the eating of honey but the climb: the rope, the height, the swarm. From the beginning the bee is bound up with danger, and honey is something taken at real risk from a defended place. Sweetness guarded by a sting is the structure underneath almost every symbolic use the insect is later put to.

The dating is genuinely uncertain. Levantine rock art of this kind has been placed anywhere from the late Mesolithic to the Neolithic, and the honey-hunter panel is usually given a broad bracket rather than a firm date.

Where scholars differ
Levantine rock art is notoriously hard to date; estimates for this panel range across several millennia.

Bees, Eland and Trance in San Rock Art

Among San (Bushman) peoples of southern Africa, honey was both a prized food and a substance with ritual weight, and bees appear in the rock art alongside the eland, the animal richest in supernatural potency. In some painted panels swarms of bee-like flecks stream around human figures.

The influential reading, developed by David Lewis-Williams and others, connects these swarm motifs to the visual phenomena of trance, the shimmering specks reported in altered states, and so to the healing dance at the centre of San religious life. On that account the bee is a marker of potency crossing between worlds, not a decoration.

This interpretation is powerful and widely taught, and it is also contested; other researchers read the same imagery more literally, as records of honey-gathering. Both readings agree that bees mattered.

Where scholars differ
The trance-vision interpretation of swarm motifs is influential but debated among rock-art specialists.

c. 7000 BCEKonya Plain, AnatoliaContestedFertility & renewal

Beeswax at Çatalhöyük

At Çatalhöyük, one of the largest Neolithic settlements known, chemical analysis of residues absorbed into cooking pots found beeswax, direct evidence that people here were already exploiting honeybee products around the seventh millennium BCE. The 2015 study that identified it traced beeswax across Neolithic Europe, the Near East and North Africa. The relationship is as old as farming itself.

A wall painting from the site has sometimes been read as a honeycomb with larvae, which would make it the earliest image of a bee colony. That reading is not secure. The same panel is also interpreted as a textile pattern or a plan of the settlement, so it is better treated as a possibility than a fact.

Where scholars differ
The 'honeycomb' reading of the Çatalhöyük wall panel is one interpretation among several.

Sugarbag and the Dreaming

Australia's native bees are stingless and store their honey in small dark pockets inside hollow trees. Across Arnhem Land and the Top End this honey, sugarbag, is a major food, and the ancestral beings associated with it are the subject of song, ceremony and painting.

Sugarbag imagery is a recognised subject in bark painting and rock art, where the internal structure of the nest is often shown in the cross-sectional style sometimes called X-ray art: the tree rendered from outside and inside at once, brood and honey pockets visible within. The bee here is a strand of country and kinship rather than an emblem standing for something else.

Egypt and the Near East

c. 3000 – 700 BCE

Where the bee first becomes a sign that can be read

from c. 3000 BCEEgyptSovereignty

The Bee Hieroglyph: Gardiner L2

Egyptian writing gave the honeybee its own sign: a compact, side-on insect with hatched wings, catalogued as L2 in Gardiner's standard list and read bit. The same word covers the bee and, in related forms, honey itself.

Because Egyptian script is pictorial, this is the first case anywhere of the bee functioning as a piece of language rather than a picture of an animal: a shape that can be carved into a name, a title, a royal formula. Everything that follows here depends on that step.

from the First Dynasty, c. 3000 BCEEgyptContestedSovereignty

He of the Sedge and the Bee

Among the great names of an Egyptian king was the title nswt-bity, written with a sedge plant and a bee and conventionally translated 'King of Upper and Lower Egypt', or more literally 'He of the Sedge and the Bee'. The sedge belongs to the south, the bee to the north, and the pairing asserts a single ruler over a doubled land. The title is attested from the First Dynasty, though the full five-name royal protocol only standardised in the Middle Kingdom.

This is the earliest surviving use of the bee as a symbol of sovereignty, and the ancestor of a line that runs through Childeric's grave goods to Napoleon's coronation mantle. A conquering emperor in 1804 and a First Dynasty king are, without either knowing it, reaching for the same insect.

Egyptologists do not entirely agree on how literally to read the pair. Whether the bee was ever a territorial badge of Lower Egypt in the way the sedge was of Upper Egypt, or whether the two signs encode an older distinction of a different kind, remains argued.

Where scholars differ
Whether the bee sign functions as a geographic emblem of Lower Egypt is debated.

Bees from the Tears of Ra

A passage in the Salt Magical Papyrus (Papyrus Salt 825) gives the bee a divine origin: the sun god Ra weeps, his tears fall to the earth, and where they land they become bees. The bees then set to work: building comb, visiting flowers, making wax and honey.

The myth does two things at once. It makes honey a literal secretion of the divine, which is why honey appears so persistently in Egyptian offering lists and temple ritual; and it makes the bee's labour a continuation of the sun's own act. That makes the insect a residue of god, not just a useful animal.

c. 2400 BCE, Fifth DynastyAbu Ghurab, EgyptIndustry & social order

The Oldest Picture of Beekeeping

In the sun temple of Nyuserre Ini at Abu Ghurab, a relief shows the whole sequence of the beekeeper's year: a kneeling figure before a stack of horizontal cylindrical hives, smoke being blown to calm the colony, comb pressed, and honey poured and sealed into storage jars.

It is the oldest known image of managed beekeeping anywhere, and it already shows a mature technology: stacked pipe hives of the kind still used in parts of the Mediterranean and the Near East four thousand years later. Egyptian apiculture was not experimental; by the Old Kingdom it was an industry with an established form.

c. 1450 BCE, Eighteenth DynastyThebes, EgyptIndustry & social order

Honey in the Tomb of Rekhmire

The Theban tomb of the vizier Rekhmire carries one of the clearest honey-gathering scenes to survive: hives tended, combs handled, jars filled and stoppered for storage. Honey in Egypt was a luxury commodity, controlled and accounted for. There was a royal official titled sealer of the honey.

Honey also went with the dead. It appears in offering formulas, in the food left for the tomb owner's use, and in preparations connected with embalming, on the strength of properties that were understood long before they were explained: honey does not spoil.

c. 1550 BCEEgyptHealing & medicine

Honey in the Ebers Papyrus

The Ebers Papyrus is a compendium of several hundred remedies, and honey runs through it: as a vehicle for other drugs, as a sweetener to make medicine acceptable, and above all as a wound dressing applied directly to burns and open injuries.

This is the beginning of a very long continuity. Honey's antibacterial behaviour (its low water activity, acidity, and enzymatic production of hydrogen peroxide) means the Egyptian practice was sound, and medical-grade honey dressings are used in wound care today. Few symbols have kept their practical footing this well.

8th century BCESuhu, middle EuphratesIndustry & social order

The Governor Who Imported Bees

Shamash-resh-usur, an Assyrian-period governor of Suhu and Mari on the middle Euphrates, left a stele boasting of an innovation: he brought down bees that collect honey, which none of his fathers had ever seen or brought, and he settled them in the gardens of his town. He then explains, for readers who will not know, how honey and wax are obtained.

The text is the earliest explicit record of beekeeping in Mesopotamia, and its tone is revealing. Bees are exotic technology here, worth commemorating on stone next to military achievements, an imported skill rather than an ancestral one.

c. 1400 BCEHittite AnatoliaFertility & renewalProphecy & the divine voice

The Bee That Found the Missing God

In the Hittite myth of Telipinu, the god of agriculture storms off in a rage and vanishes. With him goes all fertility: grain fails, animals stop breeding, the gods themselves begin to starve. The storm god searches and fails. The sun god sends an eagle, which fails.

It is the mother goddess Hannahanna who sends a bee. The bee finds Telipinu asleep in a meadow, stings his hands and feet to wake him, and wipes his eyes and feet with wax. The god wakes furious, and further ritual is needed to calm him, but he is found, and the world starts again.

The logic is exact and often repeated later: the smallest creature succeeds where the eagle cannot, and it works through a sting followed by wax, pain and then healing. The bee is the agent of return.

c. 900 BCEJordan Valley, IsraelIndustry & social order

The Apiary at Tel Reḥov

Excavations at Tel Reḥov uncovered something no one expected: an industrial apiary inside a city, with rows of cylindrical clay hives stacked in tiers, on a scale estimated at well over a hundred hives and something like a million and a half bees. It is the oldest apiary of this scale yet found.

Morphometric study of bee remains from the hives produced the strangest result. The bees were not the local Syrian subspecies but Anatolian honeybees, from hundreds of kilometres north. That implies either deliberate long-distance importation of colonies or a trade in bees that leaves no other trace.

The find also sharpens an old philological argument. The Hebrew Bible's 'land flowing with milk and honey' uses devash, which many scholars read as date syrup rather than bee honey; Tel Reḥov shows that large-scale bee honey production was, at least, entirely possible in the period.

Deborah, the Prophetess Named Bee

Deborah, Hebrew Dəvorah, means 'bee'. She is the only female judge in the book of Judges, a prophetess who gives verdicts under a palm tree, summons Barak to war against Sisera, and then sings one of the oldest poems in the Hebrew Bible.

The name is not incidental to how she is read. A bee is small, produces sweetness, and carries a sting, and the Song of Deborah is both a hymn and a war poem that ends with Sisera's mother waiting at a window for a son who will not come home. Later commentary made a good deal of the pairing of sweetness and lethality in the name.

Out of the Strong Came Forth Sweetness

Samson kills a lion barehanded, and returning to the carcass some time later finds a swarm of bees and honey in it. He eats, tells no one, and sets the incident as a riddle at his wedding feast: Out of the eater came forth meat, and out of the strong came forth sweetness.

The image binds together death and sweetness so tightly that it has never quite come apart. Naturalists have pointed out that honeybees do not nest in fresh carrion. The story presupposes a dried, cleaned-out carcass, or belongs to the same family of belief as the Greek notion that bees can be generated from dead cattle.

It survives in a place few people notice: the green-and-gold tin of Lyle's Golden Syrup, in production since the 1880s, carries the dead lion, the swarm, and Samson's line.

A Land Flowing with Milk and Honey

The formula appears some twenty times in the Hebrew Bible, from the burning bush onward: a land flowing with milk and honey. It is a promise of a country so fertile that its produce runs.

What the honey is remains an open question. The Hebrew devash covers both bee honey and thick syrup boiled from dates or grapes, and many scholars favour the latter for the formula: a land of orchards rather than hives. The archaeology at Tel Reḥov shows bee honey was produced industrially in the region regardless.

Either way the phrase became one of the most portable images in the language, borrowed for every promised land since.

Where scholars differ
Whether devash in this formula means bee honey or date syrup is long argued.

Letters Licked in Honey

In a medieval Ashkenazi initiation rite recorded in the Sefer Ha-Roke'aḥ and related sources, a child beginning study was shown a slate or cake with the letters of the alphabet written on it in honey, and licked them off. Verses about the sweetness of God's words were recited over him.

The rite makes literal a metaphor already old in the psalms: How sweet are thy words unto my taste, yea, sweeter than honey to my mouth. The metaphor becomes the procedure. The child's first act of learning is eating honey. The custom survives in modified forms today.

Apple Dipped in Honey

At Rosh Hashanah, apple is dipped in honey and eaten with the prayer that the coming year be good and sweet. Honey cake is baked; challah is dipped in honey rather than salt through the season.

It is one of the most widely kept of all bee-related customs, and one of the plainest. There is no allegory in it and no hidden doctrine, just the oldest sweetness available used as a wish for the future.

Sūrat an-Naḥl, the Chapter of the Bee

The sixteenth sūra of the Qur'an is named an-Naḥl, 'The Bee', after two verses near its middle. It is the only chapter named for an insect, and the passage is remarkable for the verb it uses:

And your Lord inspired the bee: 'Take for yourself among the mountains, houses, and among the trees, and in that which they construct. Then eat from all the fruits and follow the ways of your Lord laid down for you.' There emerges from their bellies a drink, varying in colour, wherein is healing for people. Indeed in that is a sign for a people who give thought.Qur'an 16:68–69

The verb is awḥā, from the same root as waḥy, the revelation given to prophets. The bee is described as receiving instruction from God directly, and as following a path laid down for it. Commentators have taken this in every direction: as an argument from design, as a model of obedience, as a lesson in taking only what is good and returning something better.

The word shifāʾ, healing, in verse 69 became the anchor for honey's standing in Islamic medicine, and remains among the most frequently cited verses in Muslim discussions of diet and health.

Honey in Prophetic Medicine

Building on the Qur'anic verse, the ḥadīth collections record the Prophet recommending honey, and the genre known as al-Ṭibb al-Nabawī, or prophetic medicine, gathered these traditions into practical manuals that circulated for centuries alongside the Galenic medicine of Ibn Sīnā and al-Rāzī.

One widely transmitted account has a man come repeatedly about his brother's stomach complaint and be told each time to give him honey; when it seems not to work, the reply is that God has spoken truly and the man's stomach has lied. The story is doing theological work as much as medical, but it fixed honey's position in the tradition.

The Praise of the Bees at the Easter Vigil

At the Easter Vigil the deacon sings the Exsultet before the newly lit paschal candle. In its older and fuller forms the text turns aside from Christ and the Resurrection to praise the insect that made the candle: the wax is offered as the work of bees, apis mater eduxit: the mother bee brought it forth.

This section, the laus apium or praise of the bees, is the most sustained piece of liturgical attention any animal receives in the Western rite. The bee is praised for chastity, for industry, for producing without corruption; the wax is worthy precisely because of what made it.

Later revisions shortened the passage, and much of the bee material was trimmed from the standard Roman form, which is one reason most Catholics today have never heard it.

Saint Ambrose and the Swarm

The story told of Ambrose is that a swarm of bees settled on his face as an infant in his cradle, walked in and out of his open mouth, and left behind a drop of honey before flying off. His father read it as a sign that the child's speech would be honeyed.

Ambrose did become one of the great preachers of the Latin church, and the legend gave him his attribute: he is shown in art with a beehive or skep at his feet, and he is a patron of beekeepers and candlemakers. The same swarm-on-the-lips story was told of Plato and of Pindar, and Ambrose inherits a motif much older than Christianity.

Doctor Mellifluus

Bernard of Clairvaux was known as Doctor Mellifluus, the honey-flowing teacher, for the sweetness of his preaching and his commentaries. Pope Pius XII used the phrase as the title of an encyclical on him in 1953, eight centuries after his death.

The epithet belongs to a long medieval habit of describing good doctrine as honey and good teachers as bees: reading widely, gathering from many flowers, and producing something single and sweet. Monastic writers used the bee constantly as a figure for how to read.

c. 400 – 1600 CEChristian EuropePurity & chastity

The Chaste Bee

Ancient and medieval writers believed bees did not mate: they gathered their young from flowers, or were generated without intercourse. Wax was therefore the product of virgins, and this is one reason the paschal candle was required to be of beeswax rather than tallow.

From there the bee became an emblem of chastity, and by extension of the Virgin Mary: producing without corruption, sweetness without loss of purity. Sermons and hymns drew the parallel explicitly, and the beehive appears in Marian imagery.

The natural history was wrong, and the emblem outlived it. Once a symbol is embedded in liturgy it is not much troubled by entomology.

The Gold Bees of Malia

Two bees face each other over a globe, usually read as a drop of honey, their bodies granulated, their wings held wide, three gold discs hanging beneath. It is among the finest surviving pieces of Minoan goldwork, made by craftsmen working in granulation and filigree at a level that would not be matched in the Aegean for centuries.

It comes from a burial complex, which matters: bees on grave goods, at the start of a Mediterranean tradition that will keep associating bees with the dead and with what survives them.

Whether the insects are bees, wasps or hornets has been questioned, and whether the object is a pendant or a hair ornament is unsettled. Its importance to Minoan iconography is not.

Where scholars differ
The species depicted, and the object's exact function, are both debated.

Melissa and the Nurture of Zeus

Zeus is hidden as an infant in a cave on Crete to escape his father. He is fed by the goat Amaltheia and by bees, or by the nymph Melissa, whose name simply means 'honeybee', who brings him honey. In some versions the bees that fed him are rewarded with golden colour and unusual strength.

Honey as the first food of a god sets a pattern found across the Greek material: it is the substance of infancy, immortality and inspiration, older and purer than wine. Melissa also became the standing word for a priestess in several cults, so that the mythical nurse and a real class of religious women share a name.

Artemis of Ephesus and Her Melissae

The Artemis worshipped at Ephesus is not the huntress of the Greek mainland but something older and stranger: a tiered, ornamented cult figure covered in animals, with bees among them. Her priestesses were called Melissae, bees, and her male officials Essenes, a word ancient sources gloss as 'king bees' or drones.

The whole apparatus of the cult reads as a hive: a single female at the centre, attendants named for workers, subordinate males, and strict rules of purity. Whether the Ephesians consciously modelled the priesthood on a colony or the correspondence accumulated is not knowable, but the vocabulary is unambiguous.

What the Essenes actually did, and what the famous protuberances on the cult statue represent (breasts, eggs, bull testicles, amber ornaments) are both long-running disputes.

Where scholars differ
The role of the Essenes and the meaning of the statue's protuberances are unresolved.

The Bee on the Coinage of Ephesus

For roughly six centuries the city of Ephesus put a bee on its money. The insect is shown from above, wings spread, often with the legend E-Φ. It is one of the most consistent civic badges in the ancient world, and the longest continuous run of any bee image anywhere.

This is the bee as identity rather than as allegory: a stamp of origin and guarantee, the mark by which an Ephesian coin was known in any market from Sicily to the Levant. That a religious emblem and a monetary one are the same object tells you how completely the goddess and the city were identified.

The Thriae and the Delphic Bee

The Homeric Hymn to Hermes describes three winged sisters on Parnassus, heads dusted with white meal, who teach a kind of divination and speak truth when they have eaten honey, and lie when they have not. They are generally identified with the Thriae, bee-maidens, and they represent a form of prophecy older than Apollo's.

Delphi kept the association after Apollo took the site. Pindar calls the Pythia 'the Delphic bee', the temple was said to have been built by bees, and the oracle's prophetic speech is repeatedly figured as honey: sweet, gathered from many sources, and difficult to interpret.

The link between honey and truthful speech is one of the most durable in the ancient world, and it runs straight into the Ambrose legend and the swarms said to have settled on the lips of poets.

Souls Descending as Bees

In On the Cave of the Nymphs, Porphyry reads a few lines of the Odyssey, a cave with stone bowls in which bees store honey, as an allegory of the whole cosmos, and identifies souls descending into bodies with bees entering a hive.

He is drawing on real cult practice. Honey was used in libations to the dead and in Mithraic initiation; souls were said to be attracted by honey; the dead were sometimes called melissae. The bee's movement between an unseen interior and the open air made it an obvious figure for a soul going into and out of a body.

This is the fullest ancient statement of an association that appears in fragments everywhere else: the Malia burial, the honey-and-death of Samson's riddle, the honey poured on graves.

Aristaeus, Bugonia, and Bees from a Dead Ox

The fourth book of Virgil's Georgics is a manual of beekeeping that turns into a myth. Aristaeus, the first beekeeper, loses every colony he has; sent to find out why, he learns he is being punished over the death of Eurydice, and is told to sacrifice cattle and leave the carcasses. Nine days later bees stream out of the rotting flesh.

This is bugonia, the belief that bees can be spontaneously generated from a dead ox, taken seriously by ancient writers as agricultural technique. It is almost certainly a misreading of drone flies, whose larvae do develop in carrion and whose adults resemble honeybees closely.

Wrong as biology, it was potent as symbol: life out of death, a colony resurrected from a corpse. It stands directly behind Samson's riddle and behind a great deal of later Christian use of the bee as a figure of resurrection.

The King Bee

Aristotle studied bees closely and got a great deal right: the division of labour, the stages of brood, the fact that a colony has a single large individual at its centre. He got its sex wrong, and for two thousand years European writing referred to the king bee.

This was not a trivial error. It made the hive available as a model for monarchy, and it was used that way relentlessly: an obedient people, a single ruler, no faction. When the king was finally shown to be a queen, argued from the 1580s and settled by microscopy in the following century, the political metaphor had to be rebuilt around a female sovereign, and in England it promptly was.

Pliny gathered the Greek material into his Natural History, from where it passed into the medieval encyclopedias and stayed.

Mellona, Goddess of Bees

The Romans had a minor deity for almost every agricultural operation, and bees had Mellona (or Mellonia), named from mel, honey. Augustine lists her, with some contempt, among the crowd of small Roman gods in The City of God, which is how we know about her at all.

Almost nothing survives of her cult. She is worth recording because she marks a category difference: in Egypt, Anatolia and Greece the bee is attached to a major deity or to sovereignty itself, while in Rome it gets a functionary. The Roman bee is agricultural before it is sacred.

Madhu-vidyā, the Honey Doctrine

The second chapter of the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad contains the madhu-vidyā, the honey doctrine, which proceeds by a formula repeated through every element of the world: this earth is honey for all beings, and all beings are honey for this earth.

The point is mutual constitution. Honey is made from many flowers and cannot be traced back to any one of them; in the same way each thing is the concentrated product of everything else. It is one of the earliest statements of the Upaniṣadic identity of self and world, and it is built entirely on how a hive works.

Madhu, honey, is one of the oldest words in the Indo-European inventory, cognate with Greek methu and English mead, and the Ṛgveda is already full of it: the Aśvins drive a honey-carrying chariot, honey is poured in offering, and inspired speech is honeyed.

Bhramarī Devī, Goddess of the Black Bees

In the Devī Māhātmya tradition the goddess takes the form of Bhramarī, 'she of the bees', to destroy the demon Aruṇāsura, who cannot be killed by any conventional weapon. Innumerable black bees issue from her body and tear him apart.

The bee here is the goddess's army: the small, collective, unstoppable force, effective exactly where a single hero is not. It is the same logic as the Hittite bee that succeeds after the eagle fails, arrived at independently.

Bhramarī also gives her name to bhramarī prāṇāyāma, the humming-bee breath used in yoga, in which the practitioner produces a sustained bee-like hum.

Kāmadeva's Bowstring of Bees

The Hindu god of desire carries a bow of sugarcane strung with a line of living honeybees, and shoots arrows tipped with flowers. Every element is chosen: sweetness, the hum, the flower, and the sting hidden inside all of it.

The image compresses an entire theory of desire into a weapon. What it fires is beautiful and what it carries is a wound, which is precisely what Sanskrit love poetry says about being struck by Kāma.

  • Kamadeva
  • Source image on Wikimedia Commons
Purāṇic traditionIndiaContestedSacred sweetness & wisdom

Viṣṇu as the Blue Bee on the Lotus

Viṣṇu is described in some Purāṇic and devotional texts as a blue bee resting on a lotus, and Kṛṣṇa is shown with a bee on his forehead or hovering near him. The bee on the flower is a standing figure in Indian devotional poetry for the soul drawn to God, and for God drawn to the devotee.

A related but separate matter is the epithet Madhusūdana, applied to Viṣṇu and Kṛṣṇa. It means 'slayer of Madhu', a demon of that name, and although madhu does mean honey, the title is not itself a honey epithet. The overlap has caused a good deal of confusion in popular writing.

Where scholars differ
Madhusūdana refers to the demon Madhu, not to honey, despite the shared word.

  • Vishnu

c. 5th century BCE traditionVaiśālī, IndiaSacred sweetness & wisdom

The Monkey's Gift of Honey

A monkey takes the Buddha's begging bowl, fills it with honey, and returns it. The Buddha accepts. The monkey, overcome, leaps through the trees, falls, and is reborn in a heaven.

The episode is counted among the eight great events of the Buddha's life and appears constantly in Pāla-period stone sculpture, usually as one panel of eight arranged around a central seated figure. It is remembered annually as Madhu Pūrṇimā, the honey full-moon day, still observed in Bangladesh and parts of Southeast Asia, when honey is offered to monks.

The moral is about the giver rather than the gift: the offering is small, the donor is an animal, and it is enough.

c. 200 BCE – presentChina and JapanIndustry & social orderHealing & medicine

The Bee in Chinese and Japanese Tradition

East Asia is the one major literate tradition where the bee does not become a central religious symbol. Honey is prominent in Chinese medicine (the Shennong Bencao Jing classes it among the superior drugs, harmless in long use) and beekeeping is well attested, with a treatise on it surviving from the Jin dynasty.

In poetry and painting the bee is mainly a figure of industry and of transience: busy among blossoms that will not last. It is a real presence in the arts without the sacred charge it carries around the Mediterranean, which is itself informative. The bee's symbolic weight elsewhere is not automatic but a product of particular histories.

Part VI

Mesoamerica

c. 300 – 1600 CE

The descending bee god and the stingless hives of Yucatán

c. 300–1500 CEYucatánContestedFertility & renewalSovereignty

Ah Muzen Cab, the Descending Bee God

The Maya deity associated with bees and honey is generally called Ah Muzen Cab, and is linked with the recurring 'Descending God' or 'Diving God' figure, shown head-down, arms back, legs up, as if plunging out of the sky. The type appears most famously at Tulum, where a temple is named for it.

Honey was central to Maya life, used in offerings and as the fermentable base of balché, the ritual drink made with the bark of the balché tree. Beekeeping had its own ceremonies and its own calendar, and the Chilam Balam books preserve references to the bee god in cosmological passages.

The identification of the diving figure specifically as the bee god is standard but not certain; the same image has been read as a descending sun, or as several distinct deities sharing a posture.

Where scholars differ
The identification of the Descending God with the bee deity is widely repeated but debated.

The Bee Almanacs of the Madrid Codex

Only four Maya codices survived the burnings of the sixteenth century, and one of them contains the most detailed pre-Columbian material on bees anywhere: a run of pages given over to beekeeping almanacs, showing deities attending hives, seasonal timings, and the rituals attached to them.

That a substantial fraction of one of four surviving books is about bees is a measure of how important the practice was. These are working documents, ritual calendars for people who kept colonies, not incidental decoration.

  • Madrid Codex (Maya)

pre-Columbian – presentYucatánFertility & renewalIndustry & social order

Xunan Kab, the Royal Lady

The Maya did not keep the honeybee, which is not native to the Americas. They kept Melipona beecheii, a stingless bee called xunan kab, the royal lady, housed in hollowed log sections stoppered at both ends and stacked in racks under a shelter.

Its honey is thinner, more acidic and more perfumed than Apis honey, and it was used medicinally and ceremonially rather than merely as food. The tradition is one of the few in this document that is measurably dying: the number of traditional jobones in Yucatán has collapsed over recent decades under competition from European honeybees, land-use change, and the loss of the knowledge itself.

Part VII

Northern Europe

c. 700 – 1750 CE

Bee law, bee mourning, and the hive as a model commonwealth

c. 7th century CEIrelandIndustry & social order

Bechbretha, the Bee Judgments

Early Irish law devoted an entire tract to bees. Bechbretha, bee judgments, handles swarm ownership, the liability of a beekeeper whose bees forage on a neighbour's land, the division of a swarm that settles on someone else's tree, and trespass by bees.

Its most quoted provision concerns a person blinded in one eye by a bee sting, who is owed the offending hive in compensation; if blinded in both, two hives. The tract also records the tradition that the neighbours whose flowers the bees have worked are entitled to a share of the honey: grazing rights, applied to nectar.

It is the earliest detailed legislation on bees in Europe, and it treats a colony as property with obligations attached, roughly as livestock. Nothing in it is symbolic; that is what makes it useful, as a baseline for how much of the rest is metaphor.

Telling the Bees

When someone in the household died, a member of the family went out to the hives, knocked, and told the bees. Often the hives were draped with black crape, sometimes turned, and given a portion of the funeral food. Marriages and births were announced too, but death was the important one.

The stated reason was that bees not told would leave, die, or stop producing. The underlying logic is that the colony is part of the household and holds a status somewhere between livestock and family, an entity owed information.

The custom is recorded across Britain, Ireland, France, Germany and colonial New England, and it survived into living memory; hives at Buckingham Palace were reportedly told of Elizabeth II's death in 2022. Whittier's 1858 poem fixed it in English literature.

The Painted Beehive Panels of Slovenia

Slovenian beekeepers painted the small front boards of their hives, and did so for two centuries. The subjects are saints and biblical scenes, but also satire and broad comedy: the devil sharpening a gossip's tongue, women dragging a man to the altar, a hunter's funeral conducted by the animals.

Several thousand survive. They are the largest body of folk painting attached to beekeeping anywhere, and they served a practical purpose: the boards helped bees identify their own hive in a long stacked row.

Slovenia's beekeeping tradition also produced Anton Janša, appointed the first royal teacher of apiculture in Habsburg Vienna in 1770; World Bee Day is set on his birthday.

The Mead of Poetry

The Norse mead of poetry is brewed from the blood of the murdered Kvasir mixed with honey. Anyone who drinks it becomes a poet or a scholar. Odin wins it by seduction, theft and shapeshifting, and carries it back to Asgard in his mouth in the form of an eagle.

Honey is the ingredient that makes inspiration drinkable, the same equation of honey with true and skilled speech found at Delphi, in the Ambrose legend, and in Sanskrit poetics.

The Norse hive imagery extends further: in Valhalla the goat Heiðrún browses on the tree Læraðr and gives mead instead of milk, and Yggdrasil itself drips honeydew.

1599EnglandIndustry & social orderSovereignty

So Work the Honey-Bees

In the first act of Henry V, the Archbishop of Canterbury answers a question about governing a kingdom while at war by describing a hive:

For so work the honey-bees, / Creatures that by a rule in nature teach / The act of order to a peopled kingdom.Henry V, I.ii

He then sets out the constitution: a king, magistrates, merchant-venturers, soldiers, masons, a civil citizenry, and 'the sad-eyed justice, with his surly hum, delivering o'er to executors pale the lazy yawning drone'. It is the fullest statement in English of the hive as a political model, and Shakespeare, writing before the queen was established as female, has a king at the centre.

The passage was quoted for three centuries as an argument for hierarchy, which is one of the things a symbol is for: making an arrangement look like nature rather than a choice.

1705 / 1714LondonIndustry & social order

The Fable of the Bees

Bernard Mandeville took the hive and inverted it. In his poem a prosperous bee society is full of fraud, vanity and greed; when the bees are granted the honesty they keep demanding, the economy collapses and the survivors retire, virtuous and poor, into a hollow tree.

The subtitle states the argument: private vices, publick benefits. Luxury and self-interest, not virtue, are what drive employment and wealth. The book was presented for prosecution as a public nuisance and read by everyone; Adam Smith argued against it, Hume and Voltaire took it seriously, and Keynes cited it two centuries later.

It marks a decisive turn. The hive stops being a picture of divinely ordered obedience and becomes a model of an economy, the same object doing entirely different symbolic work.

c. 1568NetherlandsContestedIndustry & social order

Bruegel's Beekeepers

Three masked figures handle straw skeps while a fourth climbs a tree after a bird's nest. The beekeepers' wicker face-guards make them faceless and slightly sinister, and the drawing carries an inscription in Flemish to the effect that he who knows where the nest is has the knowledge, but he who takes it has the nest.

The reading is contested: political allegory of the Netherlands under Spanish rule, moral emblem about knowledge versus possession, or documentary record of sixteenth-century beekeeping. It is unquestionably the last, whatever else it is, and one of the best visual sources for how skep beekeeping was actually done.

Where scholars differ
The drawing's allegorical meaning has never been settled.

  • Pieter Bruegel the Elder

medieval – 19th centuryBritain and northern EuropeIndustry & social order

Skeps and Bee Boles

The straw skep is the shape most people still draw when asked to draw a beehive, and it is obsolete. It has no removable frames, so harvesting the honey generally meant destroying the colony, driving or killing the bees with sulphur, until movable-frame hives spread in the nineteenth century.

Skeps were kept in bee boles: recesses built into a south-facing garden or orchard wall, sheltering the hive from rain and wind. Several hundred survive in Britain and are catalogued.

That the emblematic beehive on flags, coins and coats of arms is a skep dates the symbolism precisely. The image froze in the eighteenth century and the technology moved on without it.

Part VIII

Crowns and Coats of Arms

c. 480 – 1900 CE

How a small insect became an emblem of absolute power

buried 481/482 CE, found 1653TournaiContestedSovereigntyThe soul & the dead

The Gold Bees in Childeric's Grave

In 1653 a workman in Tournai uncovered the grave of Childeric I, the Merovingian king who died in 481 or 482, father of Clovis. Among the goods were around three hundred small gold objects inlaid with garnet, shaped like insects, apparently sewn onto a cloak or harness.

Chifflet published them in 1655 as bees, and as bees they entered European symbolism. Most modern scholars think they are cicadas, or stylised insects of no determinate species, and some read them as horse-harness ornaments rather than cloak decorations.

It hardly mattered. The identification as bees was made, published, and available, and a century and a half later it would give a new emperor exactly the ancient French emblem he needed. Most of the originals were stolen from the Bibliothèque nationale in 1831 and melted down; a handful survive.

Where scholars differ
Whether Childeric's insects are bees or cicadas is unresolved; the bee reading comes from Chifflet in 1655.

1804FranceSovereigntyIndustry & social order

Napoleon's Bees

For his coronation in 1804 Napoleon needed an emblem that was French, royal, and emphatically not Bourbon. The fleur-de-lis was unusable. Childeric's bees offered something better: an emblem older than the Bourbons, older than the Capetians, reaching back to the first dynasty of French kings.

Gold bees were scattered across the imperial mantle, the throne carpets, the hangings, the livery and the arms. The reasoning was partly antiquarian and partly practical: a bee is roughly the size and shape of a fleur-de-lis, and existing Bourbon textiles could be adapted.

The bee also carried the meanings Napoleon wanted: industry, order, a single ruler, and a swarm that works. It remains the most successful piece of political rebranding in the history of European heraldry, and it rests on a seventeenth-century antiquarian's guess about some garnet insects in a Belgian grave.

The Barberini Bees Over Rome

The Barberini family bore three bees. When Maffeo Barberini became Pope Urban VIII in 1623 the bees went up all over Rome: on fountains, façades, tombs, and most conspicuously on Bernini's bronze baldachin over the tomb of St Peter, where they climb the twisted columns.

The family had earlier borne horseflies and upgraded to bees on rising, which tells you how deliberately the emblem was chosen: industry, sweetness, order, and a nod to the papal role as producer of spiritual honey.

Urban's building programme stripped bronze from the Pantheon portico for the baldachin, producing the Roman line quod non fecerunt barbari, fecerunt Barberini: what the barbarians did not do, the Barberini did. His bees are still everywhere in the city.

The Masonic Beehive

Freemasonry took the skep as an emblem of industry, and it appears on tracing boards, aprons, certificates and hall decoration from the eighteenth century on. The lecture attached to it recommends the bee as a reproach to idleness and a model of co-operative labour.

Masonic use is the main channel by which the beehive travelled from religious and royal contexts into ordinary civic and commercial symbolism: savings banks, friendly societies, insurance marks, trade-union banners. Where a nineteenth-century institution wanted to say thrift and hard work, it put up a skep.

from 1847UtahIndustry & social orderSovereignty

Deseret, the Beehive State

When the Latter-day Saints reached the Salt Lake valley they named their provisional state Deseret, a word the Book of Mormon glosses as 'honeybee'. The beehive became the emblem of the settlement and stayed: it is on the state seal, the state flag, the highway signs, and the Beehive House where Brigham Young lived.

The choice was programmatic. The hive stood for co-operative labour under unified direction, which is a fair description of how the territory was actually organised, with irrigation, settlement and industry all directed centrally. Utah is still the Beehive State.

It is the one case in this document where a modern polity took the bee as its founding image and built the society to match rather than the other way round.

1842 – presentManchesterIndustry & social order

The Manchester Worker Bee

Manchester's coat of arms, granted in 1842, carries seven bees on the crest: the industrial city as a hive, its mills the combs, its people the workers. Bees were worked into the Town Hall floor mosaics, the lamp-posts, the bins, the bollards.

For a century and a half it was a piece of municipal decoration most residents walked past without thinking. That changed after the Manchester Arena bombing in May 2017, when the worker bee was adopted almost overnight as a symbol of the city's solidarity, tattooed in enormous numbers, with parlours donating the proceeds to the victims' fund, and painted across the city.

It is the clearest modern example of the process this whole document describes: a symbol lying dormant, then activated by an event, and meaning something quite different afterwards.

Part IX

The Modern Bee

c. 1900 – present

From literary hive to environmental sentinel

1901Belgium / FranceThe soul & the deadIndustry & social order

The Life of the Bee

Maeterlinck's La Vie des abeilles is part natural history and part metaphysics, built around what he called the spirit of the hive, the intelligence that appears to govern a colony although it resides in no individual bee.

The book was enormously popular and shaped a century of writing about bees, including a good deal of the language later used for emergence, superorganisms and swarm intelligence. It also drew a famous plagiarism charge: Maeterlinck later published a book on termites drawing heavily on Eugène Marais's work without adequate credit.

He won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1911. The bee book is the reason most general readers of the period had any picture of a colony at all.

1904–1917Sussex, EnglandIndustry & social orderSacred sweetness & wisdom

Sherlock Holmes Keeps Bees

Conan Doyle retired his detective to a small farm on the Sussex Downs to keep bees, and had him produce a Practical Handbook of Bee Culture, with some Observations upon the Segregation of the Queen. The choice is deliberate: bees give Holmes a closed system of rules to observe, and the hive is the nearest thing in nature to a problem.

It has become one of the standard shorthands of English fiction: beekeeping as the occupation of the retired analytical mind, taken up afterwards by a long line of imitators.

  • Arthur Conan Doyle

Plath's Bee Sequence

Plath's father was an entomologist who wrote a monograph on bumblebees; she took up beekeeping herself in Devon in 1962. In October of that year she wrote five poems ('The Bee Meeting', 'The Arrival of the Bee Box', 'Stings', 'The Swarm', 'Wintering') that she intended to close Ariel.

The sequence uses the hive for everything at once: the queen as a self that might be destroyed or might rise, the box of bees as something owned and feared, the colony overwintering as bare survival. 'Wintering' closes the group, and would have closed the collection, on the question of whether the hive survives to spring.

It is probably the most sustained serious use of the hive in twentieth-century poetry, and it takes the ancient association of the bee with the female sovereign and the soul and makes it entirely personal.

  • Ariel (poetry collection)
  • Sylvia Plath

The Bee as Environmental Sentinel

In the winter of 2006–07 American beekeepers began reporting colonies whose workers had simply gone, leaving the queen, the brood and the stores behind. Named colony collapse disorder, it produced a decade of alarm, research and campaigning, and its causes are now generally understood as a combination of Varroa and the viruses it vectors, pesticides, poor nutrition, and the stresses of migratory pollination, rather than any single agent.

The bee thereby acquired its newest symbolic role: the indicator species, the canary, the small creature whose collapse announces a larger one. 'Save the bees' became one of the most recognisable environmental slogans in the world.

There is an irony in it. The managed western honeybee is a livestock animal and is not endangered; the species genuinely in trouble are the wild and solitary bees, which have almost none of the honeybee's symbolic pull. The emblem is doing conservation work partly on behalf of insects it competes with.

A Note on East Africa

East African beekeeping sits slightly outside the symbolic lineage traced above, largely because the sources are oral rather than written, and the surviving record is thinner as a result. What is well attested is practical and social: log and bark hives hung in trees, honey used in medicine and in brewing, and honey and honey beer appearing in marriage negotiations and ceremonial exchange across many communities in the region.

The region also has its own stingless bees, Meliponula species, kept and harvested in several African countries, with a strong parallel to Mesoamerican Melipona practice: small hives, small yields, honey valued mainly as medicine.

The honest position is that this is the part of the story least well served by the written record, not the part where least happened.
